> **Working draft.** The physics below is real, and the Dreo figures come from Dreo's catalogue and independent reviews. None of it is my own measurement yet, and the long-range numbers are model extrapolations, not data. I would rather publish the method early than polish it in private.

I want a fan that moves air across a room without sounding like one. The marketing numbers (CFM, dB at some unspecified distance) do not say much about how it will *feel* three metres away. A cleaner way to think about it is to treat the fan outlet as a **turbulent jet** and ask what physics sets how far it carries.

## Momentum wins, viscosity barely matters

Compare the two effects with the jet Reynolds number, `Re = U·d/ν`. For a 12 cm outlet and air with ν ≈ 1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s:

<Chart type="bar" title="Jet Reynolds number for a 12 cm outlet" series={[{ name: 'Re', data: reData }]} yLabel="Re" logY yMin={3} caption="Laminar pipe flow breaks down near Re ≈ 2,000. Even a gentle 1 m/s outlet is already far past it, so the jet is turbulent and its spreading is set by momentum, not by the air's viscosity." />

In that regime the far field of a round jet is self-similar and, usefully, **independent of Reynolds number**. The centreline speed falls as

<p class="eq">u_c(x) ≈ B·d·U / x, with B ≈ 6.2</p>

so what matters is the product of outlet diameter and outlet speed, i.e. the momentum flux.

<Chart type="line" title="Centreline speed vs distance (12 cm outlet)" series={decay} xLabel="distance from fan (m)" yLabel="air speed (m/s)" caption="Beyond about six diameters the jet decays as 1/x. A 5 m/s outlet is already down to ≈0.6 m/s at 6 m." xMarks={[{ x: 0.72, label: '6 d' }]} />

## The trade that makes a fan quiet

Thrust (momentum flux) is `T = ρ·(π/4)·d²·U²`. Hold it fixed and the throw, which scales as `d·U`, **does not change with diameter at all**: a bigger, slower outlet delivers the same punch down the room. Noise, however, rises steeply with speed. Using a rough `U⁶` scaling for broadband fan and jet noise as a stand-in:

<Chart type="line" title="Same thrust (0.5 N), different outlet diameters" series={trade} logY xLabel="outlet diameter (cm)" yLabel="value" caption="Doubling the diameter from 12 cm to 25 cm drops the required exit speed by half and the relative noise by roughly two orders of magnitude, for the same far-field push. The exponent is a rule of thumb; real blade and motor noise add tonal components on top." />

Here is the same idea as something you can play with. The particle jet is the far-field model above, with the spreading rate of a real turbulent round jet. Hold the thrust constant and drag the diameter: the yellow line (where the breeze drops to a just-noticeable 0.5 m/s) barely moves, while the noise figure falls away.

<JetExplorer d={0.12} U={5} />

So the shopping heuristic is: **maximise outlet area for the thrust you need**, then check the motor and blades are not adding their own whine.

## The real question: getting the cold air down the hall

The free-jet picture says what a fan can do in open air. Our actual problem is a house: one split system on the dining room's north wall, and bedrooms down a hallway that the cold air never seems to reach. So I took the floor plan from the [digital twin of the house](/blog/home-digital-twin/), with its walls, doorways, kitchen joinery and the default furniture layout, and ran a 2D airflow simulation on it, right here in the page.

<HouseAirflow preset="gap" />

What it shows, averaged over the second half of 45 simulated seconds (AC at 3.5 m/s, fan at 4.5 m/s):

| Where | AC only | Dreo through the kitchen gap | Dreo around the retreat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dining | 0.45 m/s | 0.76 m/s | 0.62 m/s |
| Living | 0.13 m/s | 0.52 m/s | 0.38 m/s |
| Retreat | 0.005 m/s | 0.76 m/s | 0.79 m/s |
| Hall | 0.003 m/s | 0.71 m/s | 0.46 m/s |
| Bedrooms | about 0 | about 0.01 | about 0.01 |

Three things jump out.

1. **On its own, the split system's air stays in the dining and living room.** The jet hits the floor-to-ceiling obstacles (fridge, island, sofa) and curls back on itself. The hall is effectively dead air.
2. **One fan in the right place changes that completely.** Pointed south through the gap east of the kitchen, it sets up a loop: down through the gap, west along the hall, back up through the retreat into the dining room, where the AC tops it up again. The retreat route works too, but more of its push is spent in the retreat itself.
3. **Bedrooms are dead ends.** Even with every door open, almost nothing flows *through* a bedroom, because there is nowhere for the air to come out. A second fan in the hall aimed straight into the Bed 2 doorway lifts that room's average from 0.01 to 0.05 m/s, but it is a local swirl of hall air at the door rather than a supply of cooled air, and it partly fights the first fan. Getting conditioned air *into* a bedroom needs a through path (a door undercut or transfer grille plus a fan pushing in) or a head in the room. Try it yourself: choose "Place your own fan", then press in the hall and drag towards a bedroom door.

This is a 2D, depth-averaged slice, so it is honest about *where* air can go but not about the ceiling: a real split-system jet hugs the ceiling (the Coandă effect), and cold air sinks as it slows. Tall things like the fridge block most of the slice; low things like beds and the dining table only add drag. The solver is a lattice Boltzmann method with a Smagorinsky turbulence model, running in a Web Worker in your browser. The return air the split system draws in from the ceiling is modelled as a gentle sink around the head.

The catalogue "reach" figures (90 ft, 93 ft, 37 m) do not say what air speed counts as reached, so I ignore them.

### 353 vs 512: who wins, and where?

The 353 has the faster outlet (7.92 vs 7.62 m/s), so it wins right at the fan. Far away, though, the jet model says centreline speed scales with *outlet speed times outlet diameter*, which is set by momentum flux, so the bigger 512 should catch up. By how much depends on what you take the outlet diameter to be, and the two honest ways of estimating it disagree:

- **Rotor estimate.** Treat the rotor as the outlet. Then U·d is 1.41 for the 353 and 1.74 for the 512, so the 512 carries **about 24% more** speed in the far field.
- **Momentum estimate.** Use the catalogue airflow and speed instead: momentum flux ρQU is 4.5 N for the 353 and 4.7 N for the 512, which is **only about 2% more**. (The implied outlet is bigger than either rotor, which tells you the catalogue CFM includes air the jet has already entrained.)

<Chart type="line" title="353 vs 512: modelled centreline speed (not measured)" series={crossover} xLabel="distance from fan (m)" yLabel="centreline speed (m/s)" logX caption="Both estimates agree on the shape: the 353 leads inside about 1.2 to 1.8 m, then the 512 leads from there on. They disagree on the size of the lead at 7 m (1.55 vs 1.25 m/s by rotor, 1.99 vs 1.93 m/s by momentum). The crossover is a model prediction, not a measurement." />

So the 512 does beat the 353 down a long hallway, but the confident version of that claim ("24% more push") rests on an assumption the catalogue's own airflow figure contradicts. The cautious version is: **the 512 is at least as good past 2 m, and the 353 is the better value for a short throw.** At A$159.99 the 353 is also a cheap experiment.

### The graph that actually matters: air speed at 7 m vs noise

What I care about is how much breeze reaches the far bedroom for a given amount of noise. Two models have independent 1 m speed and noise measurements at several settings. Treat 1 m as still inside the jet's core (true for any outlet over ~16 cm), and project to 7 m with the momentum estimate:

<Chart type="line" title="Estimated air speed 7 m away vs measured noise" series={quiet} xLabel="noise at the reviewer's mic (dBA)" yLabel="speed at 7 m (m/s, modelled)" caption="At about 35 dBA, the 765S's 3.7 m/s at 1 m projects to roughly 1.0 m/s at 7 m, against about 0.6 m/s for the 513S at the same noise: nearly twice the useful breeze for the same sound. At full power they converge (~1.6 m/s), with the 765S being louder. Only the 513S's lowest and highest settings have measured noise, so the purple points in between are interpolated. The two reviews used different rooms and microphones, so treat cross-review dBA comparisons as rough." />

That points to an answer that looks nothing like the spec-sheet ranking. **The 765S buys its extra size as quiet headroom**: at bedroom-acceptable noise it delivers far more air at 7 m than a smaller fan run hard. The 704S/714S may be the sweet spot (owners consistently report them as quieter, and good at pushing air down hallways), but until there are measurements I cannot put them on the chart.
